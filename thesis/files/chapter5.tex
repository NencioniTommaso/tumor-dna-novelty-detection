\chapter{Experiments Conducted}\label{ch:experiments}

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:experimental-setup}

All experiments described in this chapter share a common computational pipeline.
Training data consists of cell-free DNA reads from a single healthy patient, encoded in FASTA format using the extended epigenetic alphabet $\Sigma = \{A, C, G, T, M, H\}$.
The mismatch string kernel is computed as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:kernel}, the resulting Gram matrix is cosine-normalised (Equation~\ref{eq:gram-normalize}), and a One-Class SVM with a precomputed kernel is fitted using \texttt{scikit-learn}'s \texttt{OneClassSVM} implementation.
Test sequences are scored through the asymmetric kernel matrix (Section~\ref{sec:gram-asymmetric}), and patient-level anomaly scores are obtained by aggregating per-read scores as outlined in Section~\ref{sec:mil}.

Unless otherwise noted, the kernel parameters used across all experiments are a maximum $k$-mer length $k_{\max}$ with mismatch tolerance $m$, combined through a Multiple Kernel Learning (MKL) linear ramp weighting scheme (Section~\ref{sec:mkl}).

Across all experiments, the OC-SVM hyperparameter is set to $\nu = 0.2$. This choice is motivated by both the theoretical semantics of $\nu$ and the known biology of the liquid biopsy setting. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ocsvm-formulation}, $\nu$ upper-bounds the fraction of training samples that the model may treat as outliers. In the clinical context, the training set is composed exclusively of cell-free DNA fragments drawn from healthy donors; however, even within a nominally healthy population, natural inter-individual variability, sequencing artefacts, and low-level somatic mosaicism can produce fragments that deviate from the majority distribution. Setting $\nu = 0.2$ instructs the model to tolerate up to $20\%$ of these marginal training fragments as permissible boundary violations, thereby preventing the decision region from over-fitting to noise while still enclosing the core healthy distribution.

The value is further rationalised by the expected tumoral signal strength at test time. Liquid biopsy studies (\textbf{ask Professor for references}) report that the fraction of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) within the total cfDNA pool of an oncology patient typically ranges from approximately $0.6\%$ in early-stage disease to over $50\%$ in advanced metastatic cases. A $\nu$ of $0.2$ produces a boundary that is sufficiently tight to detect the distributional shift even at the lower end of this range --- where the tumoral fraction is small and the anomaly signal is diluted among a majority of healthy-looking fragments --- yet permissive enough to avoid flagging the natural stochastic variation between healthy individuals as anomalous. In practice, setting $\nu$ much lower (e.g.\ $0.05$) would create an overly restrictive model that misclassifies healthy patients as anomalous due to minor distributional differences, while setting $\nu$ too high (e.g.\ $0.5$) would relax the boundary to the point where subtle tumoral signals, especially at low ctDNA fractions, become indistinguishable from normal variability.
\section{Experiment 1: Train-Seed Stability}\label{sec:seed-stability}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:seed-motivation}

Each healthy patient in the dataset is represented by a FASTA file containing approximately 1\,200\,000 cell-free DNA fragments.
Training the OC-SVM on the full set of available reads would be computationally prohibitive: even if we were to train only on a single healthy subject,the Gram matrix alone would require storing and factorising a $1.2 \times 10^{6} \times 1.2 \times 10^{6}$ matrix, far exceeding practical memory limits.
The pipeline therefore operates on a random subsample of 30\,000 training sequences, which represents only 2.5\% of the available fragments from a single patient.

This subsampling raises a question: \textbf{is a model trained on 30\,000 reads sufficient to capture the distribution of all 1\,200\,000 fragments?}
If the decision boundary learned by the OC-SVM varies substantially depending on which particular subset of reads is drawn, then the anomaly scores assigned to unseen patients would be unstable, and any diagnostic conclusion would be unreliable.
The seed stability experiment was designed precisely to quantify this risk.

\subsection{Protocol}\label{sec:seed-protocol}

The experiment isolates the effect of training-set randomness by fixing every other source of variation.
Specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fixed test data.}
    A set of 10\,000 sequences is sampled from Healthy~3 and an independent set of 10\,000 sequences from Healthy~4, both using a fixed random seed ($s_{\text{test}} = 42$).
    These two test sets remain identical across all runs.

    \item \textbf{Variable training data.}
    For each of 10 independent runs, 30\,000 sequences are sampled from Healthy~2 using a different training seed ($s_{\text{train}} \in \{42, 43, \ldots, 51\}$).
    Each run therefore trains on a distinct, non-overlapping (in expectation) subset of the available fragments.

    \item \textbf{Kernel and model fitting.}
    For each training subsample, the full string-kernel pipeline is executed: the symmetric training Gram matrix is computed and normalised, an OC-SVM is fitted, and the asymmetric test kernel is evaluated to produce anomaly scores for Healthy~3 and Healthy~4.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation metric.}
    Because both test patients are healthy, their anomaly-score distributions should ideally be indistinguishable.
    The degree of overlap between the two distributions is quantified via the Overlapping Area (OA) between their kernel density estimates:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:oa}
        \text{OA} = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \int \left| \hat{f}_{H_3}(x) - \hat{f}_{H_4}(x) \right| \, dx
    \end{equation}
    An OA close to 1 indicates that the two score distributions are nearly identical, which is the expected outcome if the model generalises well from 30\,000 training reads.
    An OA that fluctuates widely across seeds would signal that the training subsample size is insufficient.
\end{enumerate}

By repeating this procedure across 10 seeds and reporting the mean and standard deviation of OA, the experiment provides a direct empirical answer to whether the 30\,000-read training budget is adequate for stable generalisation.

\section{Experiment 2: Nystr\"{o}m $m$-Selection}\label{sec:nystrom-m-selection}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:nystrom-motivation}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:seed-stability}, training the OC-SVM requires the computation of the exact Gram matrix representing the pairwise similarities between all sequences in the training set. 
Because this symmetric matrix requires $O(N^2)$ memory space, passing the full matrix to the solver constitutes a severe computational bottleneck. 
For a training set of 30\,000 sequences, the resulting matrix, assuming 64-bit floating-point precision, requires approximately $7.2$\,GB of RAM. 
Pushing this to 40\,000 sequences would demand nearly $12.8$\,GB, which borders on the limits of standard workstation hardware. 

Our ultimate objective was to establish a richer, more comprehensive baseline of healthy cfDNA by increasing the training pool over 100\,000 sequences, specifically aiming for 120\,000 sequences for robust validation. 
However, an exact Gram matrix of this magnitude would require over $115$\,GB of RAM, making it strictly infeasible to evaluate without highly specialized computing clusters. 

To overcome this limitation, we opted to utilize the Nystr\"{o}m method (introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture}). 
The Nystr\"{o}m method circumvents the quadratic bottleneck by explicitly projecting the sparse sequence features into a low-dimensional dense space, reducing the computational complexity to $O(N \cdot m)$, where $m$ is the number of randomly selected ``landmark'' sequences. 
While mathematically sound, we needed to empirically verify if this approximation remained stable and faithful to the exact decision boundary within our specific mismatch string kernel space. 
Specifically, we needed to identify the optimal fraction of landmarks ($m/N$) that significantly reduces memory footprint without compromising the downstream anomaly detection capabilities.

\subsection{Protocol}\label{sec:nystrom-protocol}

To evaluate the approximation quality, we established a ground-truth baseline by calculating the exact Gram matrix for a training set of $N = 30\,000$ healthy sequences and fitting a standard precomputed-kernel OC-SVM. 
This exact model was then used to evaluate $10\,000$ test sequences ($5\,000$ from a healthy subject and $5\,000$ from a tumoral subject), yielding exact sequence-level anomaly scores.

We then executed the Nystr\"{o}m pipeline on the identical training and test sets, systematically sweeping the landmark ratio $m/N \in \{1\%, 2\%, 5\%, 10\%\}$. For each ratio, the Nystr\"{o}m features were extracted and a linear OC-SVM was fitted to the dense projection. 
To account for the stochastic nature of the landmark selection, the process was repeated across three different random seeds ($s_{\text{landmark}} \in \{42, 123, 7\}$) for each value of $m$.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:nystrom-metrics}

The fidelity of the Nystr\"{o}m approximation was assessed through two categories of metrics: intrinsic kernel approximation metrics and downstream task metrics.

\textbf{Intrinsic Metrics:} As defined in Section~\ref{sec:nystrom-metrics-theory}, these metrics measure the purely mathematical distance between the approximated matrix and the exact Gram matrix. We evaluated the Relative Frobenius Error, the Relative Spectral Error, and the diagonal errors on a sampled submatrix of $2\,000 \times 2\,000$ elements to quantify the preservation of the feature space geometry.

\textbf{Downstream Metrics} evaluate the practical impact of the approximation on the OC-SVM's behaviour. We compared the approximate model to the exact baseline across three dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spearman Rank Correlation ($\rho$):} This metric evaluates whether the approximated model ranks the test sequences by anomaly score in the exact same order as the baseline model, regardless of absolute score values.
    \item \textbf{Score Discrimination Gap:} We measured the difference between the mean anomaly score of the tumor test set and the mean anomaly score of the healthy test set. Preserving this gap guarantees that the approximated decision boundary maintains the same diagnostic separation power.
    \item \textbf{Sequence-Level ROC-AUC:} We computed the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve at the sequence level, treating all reads from the tumor patient as the positive (anomalous) class. It is crucial to clarify that \textbf{this sequence-level AUC lacks direct biological significance.} 
    Because a tumor patient's cfDNA pool typically contains only a minor fraction of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) buried within a vast majority of healthy fragments, the assumption that all sequences from the sick patient are biologically anomalous is incorrect. 
    Consequently, the absolute AUC value does not reflect true diagnostic accuracy. However, comparing the approximated model's sequence-level AUC directly to the exact baseline's AUC serves as an excellent technical indicator: if the approximated AUC perfectly matches the baseline, it demonstrates that the Nystr\"{o}m projection is highly faithful to the exact geometric decision boundary, validating its use for scaling up the training set size to 120\,000 sequences.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:nystrom-results}

The experimental results demonstrate that the Nystr\"{o}m approximation is highly stable and preserves the downstream anomaly detection capabilities of the mismatch string kernel, even at extremely low sampling fractions.

\textbf{Intrinsic Error Convergence:} The mathematical fidelity of the approximation was confirmed by the intrinsic metrics. At the smallest tested landmark ratio of $m/N = 1\%$ ($m=300$), the Relative Frobenius Error was approximately $0.005$ and the Relative Spectral Error was approximately $0.001$. As the landmark ratio increased towards $10\%$, these errors predictably trended towards zero, confirming the mathematical convergence of the approximation.

\textbf{Downstream Task Stability:} The approximation preserved the behaviour of the exact OC-SVM model across all evaluated downstream metrics. Even at a $1\%$ landmark sampling fraction, the Spearman Rank Correlation between the approximated anomaly scores and the exact baseline scores remained $\ge 0.9999$ across all random seeds. Both the Score Discrimination Gap ($\approx 32.7$) and the Sequence-Level AUC ($\approx 0.5427$) remained unchanged regardless of the landmark ratio. This demonstrates that the approximated decision boundary is geometrically identical to the exact boundary for the purposes of classification.

Based on these results, we adopted a landmark ratio of $m/N = 1\%$ for all subsequent large-scale experiments. By utilizing this fraction, scaling the training set to our target of $120\,000$ sequences requires only $m = 1\,200$ landmarks. This reduces the feature projection computational bottleneck from $O(N^2)$ to a manageable $O(N \cdot m)$ dense projection, resolving the memory limitations without sacrificing diagnostic performance.
